# An invitation to build Flipbook

**For:** Sharon "Ronnie" Ariyo-Adeoye
**From:** Victory Moks & Ayodeji
**Date:** September 2026
**Role:** Cofounder — Brand, Story & Community

---

## Before the details

Ronnie,

You have spent three years on a single question: how do you build locally and position globally at the same time, without sanding off the thing that made you interesting in the first place. You call the failure to do that the first and biggest mistake African founders make. You have said the worry keeping you up is the westernisation of the African professional identity — people optimising for global opportunity until the distinctiveness is gone.

That is the exact question sitting in the middle of our company right now. Not as a metaphor. As the live, unresolved, most important strategic problem we have.

Flipbook is a reading app built in Lagos. But the thing we have built is not really the reader or the catalogue or the rental mechanics. It is the room — the feeling of turning a page and finding that someone you know has already been here, left a mark in the margin, and waited for you to catch up.

And the room does not need permission from anyone. It does not need licences, or rights deals, or a territory negotiation. A book club in São Paulo can use it tonight. A student group in Mumbai. Four friends in Manila reading something they already own.

We have built the room. We have not built the reason people walk into it, and we have not decided how it speaks to the world. That is the work we are asking you to own — and we mean own, not support.

---

## Where we actually are

Not projections. What is true today.

**The product is live.** iOS is on TestFlight, Android is in the Play Console. **185 people have signed up, and they have formed 16 communities between them.** They are reading real books together, leaving reactions in the margins, arguing about chapters. There is a real backend, real-time infrastructure, offline reading, and a native highlighting layer we built from scratch because nothing off the shelf was good enough.

That second number is the one we would point at. Sixteen reading communities, around eleven readers each, formed on a product nobody has marketed, by people who found us through word of mouth, with no one in the company whose job is growth. The thing works. Almost nobody knows about it.

**Real communities are using it, and telling us so.** Oyinadé Balógun, who runs Lumee Book Club, is our most engaged tester. Her club uploaded *Thirteen* by Steve Cavanagh and read it together. She has sent us long, unprompted, generous emails — eight numbered suggestions in one sitting — because she cares what this becomes. Her words: *"Thank you for giving us our very own African-ized group reading space."* That is one club. There are hundreds of thousands like it, held together by nothing but goodwill and a group chat that buries the conversation by Tuesday.

**We are two people.** Victory builds the product and owns design. Ayodeji runs business, operations, and the author and publisher relationships. Bootstrapped, shipping weekly, and further along on nothing than most teams get on a seed round.

**We are mid-pivot, deliberately.** Version one was a club app where communities uploaded their own books. It taught us that people want the community layer badly — and that they kept asking one question: *"can I get books there?"* So we are adding a curated library. Readers rent licensed books affordably and read them together. Authors keep 70% of every rental.

**Someone else just proved the market.** Masobe Books, a respected Nigerian publisher, launched their own reading app this year. We read that as validation. They built a storefront for one publisher's catalogue. We are building the library and the room around it.

## The bigger thing, said plainly

Here is what we have realised, and it is the reason this role is a cofounder seat rather than a hire.

Flipbook is two layers with two completely different ceilings.

**The library is a local business.** Licensed books are territory-bound and rights-bound. We grow it market by market, deal by deal, slowly. It starts with African independent authors and public-domain classics, and it is genuinely distinctive because it starts somewhere specific — the worst catalogues in the world are the ones with no point of view.

**The room is a global product.** Reading together needs no licence. Every feature that makes Flipbook *Flipbook* — the communities, the reactions in the margins, the shared progress, the arguing about chapter eleven — works identically in Lagos, Lisbon, Jakarta and Lima. There is no permission to acquire. There is no gate.

And the territory is vacant. Kindle owns solo reading — the store, the device, the muscle memory, a decade of it. We are not fighting that and would lose if we tried. But **nobody owns reading together.** Goodreads squatted on it for fifteen years and let it rot. Fable is trying, and is American and feed-shaped. That ground is genuinely unclaimed, globally uncontested, and it happens to be the half of our product that needs nothing from anyone.

So the sentence we are aiming at is not *"Flipbook is the next Kindle."*

It is: **Kindle is where you read. Flipbook is where you read together.**

We want Flipbook to become the word readers everywhere use for that. The default. The thing a book club in any city assumes it will use, the way they assume WhatsApp for the group chat.

We are 185 users into that ambition. It is absurd and we know it. We also think it is achievable, because the mechanism that produced sixteen communities with zero marketing is a mechanism that does not care what country it is in.

What it needs is someone who can make the world understand it — while keeping the thing that makes it ours.

## Why you, specifically

We are not looking for a marketer. We could hire one. We are looking for someone whose existing life's work happens to be the exact problem this company must solve.

**Your framework already diagnoses us.** You tell African founders that the biggest mistake is failing to build locally while positioning globally early enough. Read our current positioning and you will find a company doing precisely that wrong — we have been describing ourselves as a library for African readers, which reads as *who it is for* rather than *where it is from*, and quietly tells a reader in Jakarta that we are not addressed to her. Meanwhile the half of our product that would serve her perfectly needs no licence and works today. You would not need to be briefed on this problem. You have been writing about it for three years.

**"African and global at the same time" is your question, and now it is a product question.** You have argued that Africans competing globally should sound *more* like themselves, not less — that the answer is better translation rather than less identity. Flipbook is that argument in software: a library rooted in African voices, a room open to the world, and a brand that has to hold both without collapsing into either. Nobody we could hire would care about that the way you already do.

**You can earn attention, which is the rarest thing we need.** Technext, The Geek Circle, Enterprise CEO Africa, African Tech Journal — four publications covering a three-year-old agency. You did not buy that. You built something worth writing about and told the story well enough that people wrote it. We need the world to notice a reading app from Lagos. You have already done that for yourself, which is harder.

**You have built a real community, not an audience.** HERcelerate is not a hashtag. You sponsored a young woman's training in lash technology, found out mid-programme that she could not read or write, and enrolled her in adult education. That instinct — to notice the actual need under the stated one — is the instinct our community function needs, because our entire retention story rests on community being genuine rather than performed.

**You understand the trust problem before we hit it.** You have written about building high-trust brands in a low-trust economy, where founders overdeliver just to be believed. We are about to ask people to pay for books they are used to finding free in a group chat, in markets where nobody has heard of us. That is a trust problem before it is a pricing problem, and you have thought harder about it than we have.

**And you have been a founder.** Lenora started with no capital, no plan, and a Canva flyer. You have sat in survival mode and built systems out of it anyway. We do not want an executive. We want someone who will feel the weight of this the way we do.

## What you would own

Not a task list. Three outcomes, and the authority to decide how they are met.

### 1. The story — including the hard part

Flipbook's public voice and narrative are yours. What we stand for, why we exist, why a reader picks us over Kindle or Masobe or the pirated PDF in their group chat.

The marquee problem inside this, and we think it is genuinely interesting: **how does a brand stay unmistakably from Lagos while being legible and desirable to a reader in Jakarta?** Get that wrong in the cautious direction and we are a regional app with a ceiling. Get it wrong in the ambitious direction and we are a generic global reading app with no reason to exist. We do not have the answer. We think you might be one of a very small number of people who could find it.

You are inheriting craft, not a blank page: a full brand identity and design system, a written voice and tone guide, a documented set of things we will never do. Challenge all of it. We would expect you to.

### 2. The audience — globally architected, locally won

Getting readers to Flipbook and keeping them. Waitlist, acquisition, activation, referral, retention.

The strategic call inside this is sequencing. The room scales globally for free; the library does not. So there is a real question about where we push, in what order, and how fast — and a real discipline required not to chase five markets before we have won one. We want your judgment on that, not just your execution.

You will have to learn things you have not done before: app funnels, cohort retention, store optimisation across markets. We would rather have someone with judgment who learns the mechanics than someone with the mechanics and no taste.

### 3. The community — the thing that decides whether we work

Sixteen communities formed without us trying. Nobody has yet asked *why* — who started them, how members got pulled in, what made them stick, and what would make a hundred more appear. That mechanism, understood and made repeatable, is the most valuable asset this company has, because it is the part that travels across borders without a single rights conversation.

Yours to build: the culture, the early community leaders, the relationships with book clubs and creators, the reasons a reader comes back for the next book instead of drifting.

Start with Oyinadé. She is already an evangelist and has offered to bring other communities along.

## Your first 90 days

Concrete, so the role is real rather than abstract. We would expect you to change it once you are inside.

**Days 1–30 — Learn and define.** Read everything. Talk to at least fifteen readers and five club organisers, including Oyinadé and at least two outside Nigeria. Then tell us what Flipbook actually is, in language we have not been able to find ourselves — and tell us how it speaks to Lagos and Lisbon in the same breath. Deliverable: the positioning and narrative we use everywhere afterwards.

**Days 31–60 — Build the engine.** A content rhythm we can sustain. The launch campaign. The first cohort of community leaders recruited and briefed. The founding-authors story told publicly, because those writers are taking a chance on us and deserve to be heard.

**Days 61–90 — Launch and learn.** Take it public. Then measure honestly and tell us what did not work, because that is the part most people skip.

## What we are honest about

**This is hard and it is early.** 185 users is a real start and a small number. No revenue. Bootstrapped. No marketing budget worth the name — the first year of growth comes from story, relationships and community rather than spend.

**The global ambition is a risk as much as an opportunity.** Companies die from trying to serve everywhere before winning somewhere. Our current view is global architecture, local go-to-market: build so nothing structurally stops a São Paulo club from working, but spend our energy where the loop is proven until it is genuinely proven. If you think that is wrong, we want to hear it in week one, not year two.

**We have already pivoted once and may again.** The library model is a conviction, not a certainty. You would be joining people who change their minds when evidence says to — a strength until it is exhausting.

**We ship fast and we argue.** Victory has strong opinions about brand because he is a designer who has lived in this product for a year. Ayodeji has strong opinions about anything touching partners and revenue. You would need to hold your ground. We want someone who does, and we are saying so now rather than letting it surprise you.

**The retention problem is real and unsolved.** Rentals give readers a natural exit. We believe community is the answer. We have not proved it. That is the most interesting unsolved problem in the company, and it is why your function is a cofounder seat.

## About money, plainly

There is no salary. Not a small one, not a deferred one. Victory and Ayodeji are paying for infrastructure out of their own pockets and have been for months. You should hear that in the first document rather than the third.

What it honestly means:

**What we cannot ask of you.** We cannot ask you to turn down paid work. We cannot ask for exclusivity. We cannot ask you to put Lenora second in a month when Lenora needs you. Those asks belong to companies that can pay, and we are not one yet.

**What we are offering instead.** Ownership — a real, vested, properly papered share of whatever this becomes. Founding status, your name on the company from the beginning rather than added later. Complete creative authority over brand, which is rarer than it sounds and nearly impossible to get as a hire. And two people who ship every week instead of talking about shipping.

**One more thing, because it is true.** When Flipbook launches it will be covered — we have some relationships and you have the instincts. Your name is on that story as the cofounder who built the brand, and that is fuel for Lenora rather than competition with it. We are not offering visibility as payment. We name it because it is real arithmetic and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

We know exactly what we are asking: build something valuable with people who cannot yet pay you, believing a share of it is worth more than a salary would have been. You have made a version of that bet before, with less information and nobody beside you.

## What this would ask of you

We are not going to open with a number of hours, because it is not the real question.

The real question is whether you would own the outcome — whether, when a club goes quiet in week three or a launch lands flat, it sits on your desk and you feel it. That is what separates a cofounder from a consultant, and it has never been measured in a timesheet.

**What we hope.** That this becomes one of the two or three things you are known for. Not a side project mentioned third. We are not asking you to close Lenora — Lenora is yours, it should stay yours, and a cofounder who built a business from nothing is worth considerably more to us than one who has not. But we would want Flipbook to matter to you the way it matters to us.

**What we would actually need.** Three things, none of them hours: present at the weekly review most weeks; reachable inside a day when something breaks or a decision is waiting; genuinely available through the launch window, when the work compresses.

**What we do not need.** You at a desk for forty hours. If you can land the positioning, the launch and the first communities in twenty hours a week because you are fast and have done versions of this before, that is a better outcome than someone slower taking fifty.

For what it is worth: Victory and Ayodeji are both still balancing this against other commitments. We would be poor company to demand of you what we are not yet doing ourselves.

## Equity

This is a cofounder seat and it comes with cofounder equity — meaningful, real, and properly papered.

We have deliberately not put a percentage in this document. Not to be cagey, but because the number should follow the shape, and the shape is a conversation to have *with* you rather than present *to* you. We would rather agree it once you have seen the work up close and we have all seen how we work together.

What we can tell you now, so you know this is serious:

- **Four-year vest, one-year cliff.** Standard, and it protects all three of us.
- **Acceleration on acquisition**, so you are not disadvantaged in an outcome you helped create.
- **Reviewed at twelve months**, in writing, so that if your involvement grows the equity can grow with it.
- **Documented by a lawyer** before you start. Real paperwork, not a handshake. We would rather spend money getting this right than have it become the thing that breaks a friendship in year three.

You would be a founder in every sense that matters: your name on the company, a real say in direction, and the standing to disagree with us in public.

## How we work

Small and deliberate. We ship weekly, review on Sunday evenings, and keep the thinking written down rather than in our heads — vision, product spec, roadmap, go-to-market, unit economics, all documented and all open to you from day one.

No performance theatre. No vanity metrics. We would rather hear a campaign failed on Tuesday than hear an optimistic version on Friday.

We also care about this beyond the business case. Victory is building the reading app he wished existed. Ayodeji left a stable path for it. We think reading deserves better tools than it has, and that whoever builds them properly gets to shape what a generation reads — and where they read it from.

---

## What happens next

Nothing here is decided. This is an invitation, not an offer letter, and the terms are deliberately open so you help shape them.

1. **Read this, then tell us where we are wrong.** Genuinely. If the positioning is off or the role is shaped badly, we want it before you say yes rather than after.
2. **A long conversation between the three of us** — not an interview. Your questions about direction, about us, about whether you actually want this.
3. **Two weeks of building something together.** One real problem — we would suggest the positioning brief, since it is what we most need and what you are best at. Not an audition; cofounders do not audition, peers find out whether they work well together. Nobody is paid for those two weeks, ourselves included, and if we all say yes at the end, that time counts as the beginning of your vesting rather than an unpaid preamble.
4. **Then we agree the shape and the number** — how involved you want to be, and the equity that matches it — and we paper it properly.

That order is on purpose. Terms negotiated before either side knows what they are agreeing to tend to be the terms people resent later.

## The last thing

You have said there is almost no bad story — only bad translation. That founders should take up space unapologetically, tell the story to anybody, tell it badly, and get better by doing it.

Flipbook has a good story and we have been telling it badly. Worse than badly — we have been telling it small. We have been describing a Lagos reading app when what we are building is the room where the world reads together, and we could not see the difference until recently.

You have spent three years teaching people how to be from somewhere and legible everywhere at the same time.

Come and do it for something bigger than a profile.
